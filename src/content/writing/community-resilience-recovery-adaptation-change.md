## Overview

This portfolio analysis synthesizes two graduate reflection papers on community resilience. It examines resilience as more than the ability to “bounce back” after disaster and instead treats it as an evolving process shaped by social, economic, political, cultural, and environmental conditions.

Drawing on Wilson, Masterson and colleagues, Meerow and Newell, and Battistoli, the analysis asks whether recovery should simply restore pre-disaster conditions or create opportunities to reduce vulnerability, strengthen community capacity, and improve the relationship between institutions and the people they serve.

## Core question

**Should resilience be understood as a return to pre-disaster conditions, or as a process of recovery, adaptation, and change shaped by planning, vulnerability, power, community knowledge, and competing priorities?**

## Resilience as a changing trajectory

Wilson’s transition framework presents resilience as a trajectory rather than a fixed endpoint. Communities move through changing conditions, and decisions made at critical points can strengthen or weaken future resilience.

This perspective also raises a distributional question: different groups can experience the same disruption in different ways, making it difficult to describe an entire community as uniformly resilient.

## Planning, hazards, and vulnerability

The analysis uses the disaster-management cycle of **mitigation, preparedness, response, and recovery** to examine how planning affects resilience.

Masterson and colleagues’ Disaster Impacts Model distinguishes among **hazard exposure, physical vulnerability, and social vulnerability**. The central point is that hazards alone do not determine the scale of a disaster. Outcomes also depend on the built environment, social conditions, and unequal access to resources.

## Recovery as an opportunity for change

The analysis questions whether successful recovery should mean recreating what existed before a disaster.

If pre-disaster conditions contained structural vulnerabilities, restoring the old normal can reproduce the same risks. Recovery can instead become an opportunity for adaptation, mitigation, and rebuilding in ways that reduce future vulnerability.

## Resilience for whom?

Meerow and Newell’s framework asks **who, what, when, where, and why** resilience is being pursued.

Those questions show that resilience planning involves choices about priorities, geography, timing, and resource allocation. Identifying a vulnerability does not automatically determine which need should receive priority.

## Social memory, trust, and community knowledge

The second reflection extends the analysis by examining **social memory**: the accumulation of individual histories, shared experience, traditions, learning, and stakeholder relationships within a community.

Wilson’s framework cautions against assuming that local knowledge is always beneficial. Social memory can be **benign or malign**. It can preserve useful knowledge and strengthen a community’s ability to recognize and respond to hazards, but it can also reinforce vulnerable pathways, inaccurate risk perceptions, or distrust.

The paper uses Battistoli’s post-Katrina research on environmental-risk communication in New Orleans to illustrate why public trust matters to resilience. Distrust of official risk messaging can complicate mitigation and preparedness long after the immediate disaster has passed.

Stakeholder networks matter for the same reason. Resilience planning benefits from the involvement of civic organizations, faith-based organizations, voluntary organizations, governments, and groups connected to vulnerable populations. Those relationships can help planners understand how information, resources, authority, and trust actually move through a community.

The resulting tension is important for public administration: **institutional expertise and community knowledge need one another**. A technically sophisticated plan can fail if planners misunderstand the community for which they are planning, while community knowledge alone can preserve maladaptive practices or inaccurate perceptions of risk.

## Applied example

The analysis applies these ideas to Hurricane Katrina, examining how hazard exposure interacted with social vulnerability, displacement, infrastructure loss, evacuation capacity, uneven recovery resources, and later questions of trust.

The example illustrates why recovery cannot be evaluated only by whether infrastructure is rebuilt or services return. It also requires examining who benefits, which vulnerabilities remain, whether rebuilding reduces future risk, and whether institutions have sustained credible relationships with the communities they expect to prepare and respond.

## Why this work matters

This project demonstrates graduate-level synthesis across **community resilience, disaster planning, vulnerability, social memory, public trust, equity, and public administration**.

Its central argument is that resilience cannot be measured only by a return to “normal.” A stronger assessment considers recovery processes, unequal vulnerability, planning choices, community knowledge, trust, and whether rebuilding reduces or recreates the conditions that produced harm.

For the portfolio, it adds a disaster-governance dimension while connecting directly to broader public-administration questions about implementation, resource allocation, institutional responsibility, stakeholder engagement, and equitable service delivery.
