## Overview

This graduate paper examines community resilience as more than the ability to “bounce back” after disaster. It treats resilience as an evolving process shaped by social, economic, political, cultural, and environmental conditions.

Drawing on Wilson, Masterson and colleagues, and Meerow and Newell, the analysis asks whether recovery should simply restore pre-disaster conditions or create opportunities to reduce the vulnerabilities that existed before disruption.

## Core question

**Should resilience be understood as a return to pre-disaster conditions, or as a process of recovery, adaptation, and change shaped by planning, vulnerability, power, and competing community priorities?**

## Resilience as a changing trajectory

Wilson’s transition framework presents resilience as a trajectory rather than a fixed endpoint. Communities move through changing conditions, and decisions made at critical points can strengthen or weaken future resilience.

This perspective also raises a distributional question: different groups can experience the same disruption in different ways, making it difficult to describe an entire community as uniformly resilient.

## Planning, hazards, and vulnerability

The paper uses the disaster-management cycle of **mitigation, preparedness, response, and recovery** to examine how planning affects resilience.

Masterson and colleagues’ Disaster Impacts Model distinguishes among **hazard exposure, physical vulnerability, and social vulnerability**. The central point is that hazards alone do not determine the scale of a disaster. Outcomes also depend on the built environment, social conditions, and unequal access to resources.

## Recovery as an opportunity for change

The analysis questions whether successful recovery should mean recreating what existed before a disaster.

If pre-disaster conditions contained structural vulnerabilities, restoring the old normal can reproduce the same risks. Recovery can instead become an opportunity for adaptation, mitigation, and rebuilding in ways that reduce future vulnerability.

## Resilience for whom?

Meerow and Newell’s framework asks **who, what, when, where, and why** resilience is being pursued.

Those questions show that resilience planning involves choices about priorities, geography, timing, and resource allocation. Identifying a vulnerability does not automatically determine which need should receive priority.

## Applied example

The paper applies these ideas to Hurricane Katrina, examining how hazard exposure interacted with social vulnerability, displacement, infrastructure loss, evacuation capacity, and uneven recovery resources.

The example illustrates why recovery cannot be evaluated only by whether infrastructure is rebuilt or services return. It also requires examining who benefits, which vulnerabilities remain, and whether rebuilding reduces future risk.

## Why this work matters

This project demonstrates graduate-level synthesis across **community resilience, disaster planning, vulnerability, equity, and public administration**.

Its central argument is that resilience cannot be measured only by a return to “normal.” A stronger assessment considers recovery processes, unequal vulnerability, planning choices, and whether rebuilding reduces or recreates the conditions that produced harm.

For the portfolio, it adds a disaster-governance dimension while connecting directly to broader public-administration questions about implementation, resource allocation, institutional responsibility, and equitable service delivery.
