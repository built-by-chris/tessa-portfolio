## Overview

The PACT Act expanded health-care and disability-benefit eligibility for veterans exposed to toxic hazards while also expanding the administrative responsibilities placed on the Department of Veterans Affairs. This analysis treats that change not only as a benefits policy, but as a public-administration problem involving implementation capacity, evidentiary burden, service delivery, oversight, and legal entitlement.

At the center of the paper is a structural tradeoff: presumptive service connection can make the benefits process less burdensome for eligible veterans while simultaneously increasing the volume, staffing, coordination, and accountability demands placed on the VA.

## Core question

**How does a major expansion of veterans’ benefits change the administrative burdens, institutional responsibilities, and accountability demands involved in turning statutory eligibility into actual services and benefits?**

## Analytical approach

The paper evaluates the PACT Act through four public-administration lenses:

- **Traditional managerial administration** — hierarchy, expertise, standardization, staffing, competence, efficiency, and administrative capacity.
- **New Public Management** — performance measurement, service delivery, technology, contracting, responsiveness, and customer experience.
- **Political administration** — representation, responsiveness, advocacy, congressional oversight, and the role of politically visible constituencies.
- **Legal administration** — due process, evidentiary standards, procedural fairness, equal treatment, statutory entitlement, and review.

Using the same policy across all four lenses makes it possible to distinguish operational concerns from political, legal, and institutional ones rather than treating implementation as a single-dimensional problem.

## Key findings

Presumptive service connection can reduce the individualized evidentiary burden placed on veterans and make some eligibility decisions more consistent. At the same time, expanded eligibility increases claims volume, screening obligations, staffing needs, outreach, coordination, and oversight demands.

The New Public Management lens is useful for evaluating outputs such as processing time, enrollment, staffing, and access, but those measures cannot fully capture legal rights, fairness, recognition, or due process. Faster processing is not necessarily better administration if accuracy or procedural fairness declines.

The political lens highlights the importance of advocacy, representation, and congressional oversight in transforming long-standing toxic-exposure concerns into policy. It also raises a continuing question about veterans whose exposures or conditions fall outside established presumptions.

The legal lens shows that presumptions are more than an administrative shortcut. They change the evidentiary pathway to benefits by reducing the need for covered veterans to prove an individualized causal nexus under conditions where historical exposure documentation and medical causation may be difficult to establish.

## Why this work matters

The analysis demonstrates a central challenge in public administration: a policy can reduce burden for the public while creating new burdens for the institution responsible for implementing it. The PACT Act therefore illustrates how legislative change becomes an operational problem involving capacity, fairness, timeliness, accountability, and the translation of legal rights into actual services.
