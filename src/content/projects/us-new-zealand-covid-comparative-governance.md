## Overview

This project compares the governmental responses of the United States and New Zealand during the COVID-19 pandemic. Rather than comparing health outcomes alone, it examines how political institutions, administrative capacity, public-health infrastructure, transparency, and response coordination shaped each country’s ability to act during a major crisis.

The project combines a comparative essay with two country-profile tables designed to place the pandemic response within a broader governance context.

## Comparative question

**How did differences in political structure, state capacity, preparedness, and public-health coordination shape the pandemic responses of the United States and New Zealand?**

The comparison treats each country as a distinct institutional system rather than assuming that similar democratic status would produce similar crisis-management approaches.

## Comparative framework

The country profiles examine a broad set of indicators and institutional characteristics, including:

- political system;
- freedom ratings;
- state fragility;
- information and media freedom;
- overall state capacity;
- public-health capacity;
- corruption indicators;
- COVID-19 record keeping and transparency;
- national, regional, and local response structure;
- notable pandemic-era policies;
- human rights and rule of law;
- uneven economic development;
- demographic pressures.

Using a common framework allows the project to compare not only what each government did, but the institutional conditions under which those decisions were made.

## Findings from the project

The analysis emphasizes the United States’ highly decentralized federal structure, in which public-health authority and implementation responsibilities are distributed across federal, state, and local institutions. The project contrasts that structure with New Zealand’s more centralized national response and its use of an established pandemic-planning framework.

The original project concludes that differences in timing, coordination, preparedness, and institutional structure were important to understanding the countries’ divergent early-pandemic experiences. It also recognizes major contextual differences—including geography, population density, and timing of exposure—that limit simplistic comparisons between the two cases.

## Data and source work

A substantial portion of the project involved compiling country-level indicators from multiple public and research sources into structured comparison tables.

The resulting profiles bring together measures related to governance, freedom, state capacity, public-health systems, corruption, transparency, demographic pressure, and institutional response. That component makes the project useful as more than a narrative essay: it demonstrates comparative research design, source synthesis, and the organization of heterogeneous indicators into a common analytical framework.

## Why this work matters

This project adds a comparative-governance dimension to the portfolio. It shows the ability to move across political systems, identify relevant institutional variables, organize multi-source evidence, and connect formal governmental structure to implementation during a public emergency.

It also complements later graduate work in public administration by showing an earlier interest in administrative capacity, crisis response, and the relationship between institutions and outcomes.
